## Hypothesis

Most of what a reader as good as our own net learns from our card play, it learns from which of several equal cards we chose, because the bot breaks ties by the same net the reader uses. Those choices cost nothing to randomize. Choices between cards that are merely close are a different matter: by the bot's own values, choosing evenly among cards within 0.05 tricks of the best costs 0.065 tricks a board, half of everything the reader gains from our plays, so they are not worth randomizing except in kinds of decision this experiment has to find. Predictions, not known values: (1) with our choices among equals made at random, a calibrated reader keeps less than half of the +0.124 tricks a board it gains from reading the opponents' plays today; (2) a reader that keeps reading them with today's net is misled and loses more than it gained (the Gen 8 session measured about -0.36 IMPs for a full-weight reader against a bot that always plays the highest of equal cards); (3) declarer hiding its equals is worth more than defenders hiding theirs, because declarer has no partner who reads them.

## Mechanism

Max, 12 Nov 14:53 UTC: "We should consider information leakage from our plays. I am worried that the scorer and policy net is too good. We need to sort out how much to randomize to make ourselves harder to read... The basic principle is to randomize close decisions, but how close and which type needs analysis."

Three quantities for each kind of decision: how much it tells a reader (nats, and tricks through the bench), what randomizing it costs the player (tricks on the true deal), and what it costs partner, who reads us too. A kind of decision is worth randomizing when the first exceeds the other two.

Kinds:
- **E, equals.** Cards that are the same card for the play: touching cards in one hand once the cards between them are gone (spots, and honours such as K from AK). Free double dummy.
- **S, same value.** Different cards the bot's weighted solves rate exactly equal (which suit to discard, which of two safe exits). Free by the bot's own estimate.
- **C, close.** Within eps of the best by the bot's own values, eps from 0.02 to 0.3 tricks.
- **Calls.** A call whose runner-up the bidder rates nearly as likely.

## Primary metric and population

Part 1, offline on p41's published pools (530 house tables, 21,417 decisions, v4; double-dummy tricks a board, deal as the unit):
- **1a, done (log, 14:56):** what the reader gains from each thing it reads, by who reads; and the cost of choosing evenly within eps.
- **1b:** the reader's gain when our choices among equals carry nothing: the net's probability of the class of the card (the sum over the equal cards the hand holds in that layout) in place of the card's, with the choice inside the class even. On recorded tables this is exactly what a calibrated reader would gain against a version of us that randomizes inside the class, since the class reading does not look at which equal card was played. Classes: (i) spots nine and below of the suit (E9-3's class), (ii) every set of touching cards, honours included. Separately for declarer's plays read by defenders, defenders' plays read by declarer, and partner's plays.
- **1c:** the same for S: the reading with the net's probability summed over the cards the chooser's own stored solves rate exactly equal (the chooser's decision is in the pools too). This one is approximate: in another layout the chooser would hold other cards and other ties.
- **1d:** for C, by kind of decision (a discard, a lead to a new trick, second hand, third hand, which honour to win with), the cost of randomizing within eps beside the reader's gain from that kind of play.
- **1e, calls:** how often the house bidder's second call has more than a third of the first's probability; the scorer's reading with and without those calls; the double-dummy cost of taking the second call (bill).

Part 2, played boards, only for policies that part 1 puts above 0.03 tricks a board:
- a bot that hides equals as declarer only, and one that hides them in every seat, each against `8p-32` reading at full weight (a reader that has not adapted) and against a reader told that equals are random (a reader that has). Same deals; 1,200 boards a pair.

## Regression slices

Partner's side of it: what our own defence loses when partner's equals say nothing. BEN as the reader of us is not measurable here (we do not hold BEN's reading), so the reader is our own net throughout; say so.

## Compute cap

## Stopping rule
